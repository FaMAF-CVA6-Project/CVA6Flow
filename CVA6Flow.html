What it shows

CVA6Flow draws how the CVA6 core ran a RISC-V program, cycle by cycle. It reads a JSON that CVA6Flow_tracer.py writes from a Verilator VCD of the core. Each row is one instruction, in the order it was fetched, and each column is one cycle, so a row runs left to right through the pipeline: fetch, decode, issue, execute and commit. A long row is an instruction that waited, and its colours say what for.

Loading a JSON

Load JSON opens a panel that lists the JSONs of a folder. Click Choose a Folder… once and pick the folder that holds your runs: every JSON under it is listed by subfolder, with its date and size, and a click loads one. The filter box keeps the JSONs whose folder and name hold every word you type, so ddr3 daxpy finds daxpy in every folder with ddr3 in its name. ↑ and ↓ choose, Enter loads and Esc closes.

The next Load JSON opens the list where the last JSON came from, until the page is reloaded. A browser lets a page read a folder only while the page stays open, so after a reload you choose the folder again, which also lists any JSON written since. Nothing is uploaded, even where the browser asks as if it were: the page reads only the JSON you click. A JSON past the whole-file limit is marked, since the viewer counts its instructions first and may ask for a range of them.

Open a Single File… in the panel is the browser's own dialog, and a JSON dropped anywhere on the page loads too. Before anything is loaded, the start page also offers the sample JSONs that come with the viewer.

Moving around

Drag the diagram to move it, or use the scrollbars and the wheel, with Shift for sideways. ← and → move one cycle, ↑ and ↓ one row, Page Up and Page Down a screen of rows, and Home and End jump to the first and the last cycle.

Zoom X sets how many pixels wide a cycle is: drag its slider, type a number in the box and press Enter, or press + and -. Fit All, or 0, fits the fetch range to the window's width and keeps the row at the top. Zoomed in far enough, each cell is tagged with its stage.

Fetch Range shows only the instructions from one fetch number to another, and Reset shows them all again. Main Code, or G, shows the measured part of the program, between the two statistics markers of the test templates, leaving out the start-up code. The ruler counts cycles from 1 at the first cycle of the range shown. Find PC, above the labels, finds instructions by address, and Enter and Shift+Enter step through the matches.

Reading the diagram

Each cell covers the cycles the instruction spent in one stage or one wait, and cells grow taller towards commit. From the left: the Fetch1 request to the I-cache, the Fetch2 response that brings the line, Decode, Issue into the scoreboard, Execute in the colour of the unit that runs the instruction, and Commit. A lighter cell between them is a wait: in the front-end FIFO, in the decode register before issue, tagged (mem) while an operand is still coming from a load and (reg) from another instruction, or finished and waiting to commit behind an older instruction. The legend above the diagram lists every colour the range draws, and holding the pointer over an entry says what it is.

A badge on a cell says how a branch went. On Fetch2, T✓ or N✓ is a prediction that stood, taken or not taken. On Execute, T✗ or N✗ is a mispredicted branch and T! a taken branch the predictor made no prediction for. Either redirects the front end, and the younger instructions fetched on the wrong path are flushed: drawn in a faded red, with a red tick and a ✗ at the end of the cycle they were thrown away in. The instruction fetched next first waits through a bubble, the refetch, in the colour of its cause: blue after a predicted taken branch, amber after an unpredicted one, red after a misprediction and teal after a flush, such as a fence's or a CSR write's.

Forwarding, under View, draws an arrow from an instruction's writeback to the issue of one that used its result: teal when the value came straight from writeback in that cycle, purple when it was read from the scoreboard, written back earlier. Stalls, also under View, tints every cycle in which some instruction waited. A load or store shows a strip over its Execute cell for its time in the load-store unit, ld✗ or st✗ when it missed the D-cache, then its D-cache stall. An atomic does its memory access at the head of commit, and a fence holds commit while the D-cache is flushed, each in a cell of its own before Commit. A dashed outline marks fetch cycles the tracer filled in because the I-cache never answered.

The label column gives each row its fetch number, its address and the instruction, coloured by what runs it: green for integer, blue for floating point, amber for memory, violet for branches and a dim red once flushed. ▪ marks a compressed instruction and ↪ one whose bytes span two fetch lines. Drag the column's right edge to widen it.

The tooltip and pins

Hold the pointer over a row for its tooltip: the cycle of each stage, every wait with its length, the stall it adds, how a branch went and what a load or store did. A (max N) beside a wait is the longest of that wait in the range, and at the bottom the stage indicator names the cell under the pointer. Tooltip in the toolbar, or T, turns it on and off.

P pins the tooltip into the dock along the bottom, beside those pinned before, so two instructions can be read side by side. A card's title, every # number and every (max N) in it go to their instruction. Hide keeps the cards out of the way, as Esc does, Clear All removes them, and dragging the dock's top edge resizes it.

Metrics and Extra Info

The metric bar along the bottom counts the current fetch range: its cycles, the instructions fetched, committed and flushed, the cache misses and accesses, the branches and how they went, the time at an assumed 50 MHz clock, the IPC and the stalls. Hold the pointer over a figure for what it counts. Its first fields follow the pointer, the cycle, the instruction, its unit and its address. Copy Metrics, or C, copies every figure, one per line.

The stall figures count cycles in which some instruction waited, each cycle once however many waited in it. Flush stall is the refetch after a mispredicted or unpredicted branch or a flush: the bubble and the wait before the next fetch. Fetch stall is an instruction waiting in the front end: for the I-cache, in the front-end FIFO, or in the decode register only for its turn to issue. Data stall is an instruction held in the decode register for an operand, or a load or store waiting for the load-store unit to take it. Data-memory stall is a load waiting on a D-cache miss for data a later instruction in the range reads. Stall counts a cycle when any of them does, so it is never more than their sum.

Extra Info, or I, describes the configuration the JSON was run with and the range in more detail: the statistics, the functional units, the caches, the branches, the memory writebacks and how long their AXI writes took. Its Where to look section links the instruction that waited longest for each wait and those of each rare event, such as a mispredicted branch, and a link closes the panel and goes to its row. Its stall diagram draws when each kind of stall happened across the range.

Memory writebacks

The track under the diagram draws each dirty D-cache line written back to memory, one lane per flush slot: a light bar while the line waits to be sent, a full bar from the AXI write to its acknowledgement, numbered with the line, and a thin line where the eviction that caused it happened. Hold the pointer over a bar for its details. Hide Writebacks under View, or W, hides the track, and dragging its top edge makes the lanes taller.

View and Export

View, or V, holds what the diagram draws, Forwarding and Stalls. It hides the metric bar, the labels and the writeback track, switches between the dark and light themes, as L does, and has Figure Mode, F, which leaves the diagram alone on the page for a screenshot, and Reset View, R, which puts every choice back but the theme. The page remembers these choices in this browser.

Screenshot, or S, saves the window as a PNG at three times its resolution, and the key keeps the tooltip under the pointer in it. Export saves the diagram alone as a PNG, as E does, with the metric bar or a see-through ground if you ask, or every instruction of the range as CSV for a spreadsheet or pgfplots.
